New to campaign finance? How the money works (3-minute read)

A federal campaign is paid for by several kinds of organizations at once. Each has different rules on who can give, how much, whether it can work with the candidate, and when the public finds out. This page tracks all of them from FEC filings. Tap any ? or dotted-underlined term for a definition.

Why the party numbers look small

Parties used to do much of their spending as independent expenditures, which are reported within a day or two. Since the Supreme Court's June 2026 ruling in NRSC v. FEC, parties can spend without limit in coordination with their candidates. That spending shows up only on the party's regular reports. So party money can look like it's drying up when it's really moving somewhere that's reported later. The yellow banner at the top gives the date that coordinated-spending data runs through.

Why candidate money matters too

Outside groups and campaigns buy the same ads at different prices. Stations must sell candidates airtime at the lowest unit charge, while super PACs pay market rates that spike before Election Day. A party that routes support through its candidates, instead of through super PACs, looks weaker on outside-money charts than it really is. That's why each race shows the nominees' own fundraising beside outside money. Races where the two point in opposite directions carry a split tag.

How to read this tracker

  1. Start with the header. Offense share tells you whether each party is attacking or defending. Races in play tells you how wide each party's map is.
  2. Watch the new-races feed. A first $100K, a first party dollar or a new split signal often means a race just joined someone's target list.
  3. Sort the table by "Last 7 days" to see where money is moving now.
  4. Open a race to see who is spending, the nominees' own money, and links to the original filings on fec.gov.
  5. Remember what's missing. Spending shows where groups are placing bets, not why, and not how well the ads work.

    How it's counted. Spending for a Democrat or against a Republican helps Democrats, and the reverse helps Republicans. Primaries, 2024 leftovers and third-party candidates are dropped. "Party" means party committees by FEC committee type (X/Y); everything else is outside money. Offense is money helping a party in a seat the other party holds now. Races where redistricting makes the holder unclear are tagged and left out of offense share. Amended filings replace the originals; a notice that later shows up on a periodic report is counted once. Single items above $5M (House) or $25M (Senate) are held until the FEC's processed data confirms them.

    Candidate money. Party committees can now route money through coordinated spending, and candidates buy TV at the lowest unit rate, so outside money alone can understate a party. Each race shows its nominees' own fundraising and cash on hand, cycle to date, from the FEC's candidate summary file. The nominee is the candidate general-election outside money targets most (at least $10K). Otherwise it's the top fundraiser, marked as inferred. A split tag means outside money favors one party while nominee fundraising favors the other, each by at least $250K; it only fires when both nominees were identified from general-election spending and both have a report on file. With an API key, nominee totals update from raw e-filed reports the day they're filed (ahead of FEC processing), and from Oct. 15 to Nov. 1 large contributions ($1,000+) from 48-hour notices are added on top of the last report. Races with a note tag have context the filings alone don't show. Candidate money is kept out of offense share: it shows where donors are betting, not where party strategists are.
